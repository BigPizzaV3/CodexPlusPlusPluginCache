## 1. Establish the brief

Use details already supplied. Ask only for missing information that materially changes the output, such as purpose, audience, duration, tone, language, speaker count, or required call to action. If the user wants speed, make reasonable assumptions and state them briefly.

## 2. Write for the ear

- Use natural spoken phrasing, short sentences, and clear transitions.
- Put the main idea early and remove filler.
- Match vocabulary, energy, and formality to the audience.
- Never invent product claims, testimonials, prices, guarantees, or legal facts.
- Preserve required facts, names, pronunciations, and calls to action.

## 3. Control timing

Use these planning ranges unless the user supplies a pace:

- Calm or instructional: 120–140 words per minute.
- Conversational: 140–160 words per minute.
- Energetic advertisement: 160–180 words per minute.

Estimate duration from the final word count and pace. Treat the estimate as approximate because pauses, emphasis, and speaker delivery affect runtime. If the target is strict, revise the script to fit rather than merely reporting an overrun.

## 4. Add production direction

When useful, include concise bracketed cues such as `[warm]`, `[pause]`, `[whisper]`, or `[emphasize: phrase]`. Do not overload every sentence with cues. For multiple speakers, label every turn consistently. Add a pronunciation list for unusual names, brands, abbreviations, or technical terms.

## 5. Deliver the result

Use the smallest structure that serves the request:

1. Final script.
2. Estimated word count and duration.
3. Voice and delivery direction.
4. Pronunciation notes, when needed.
5. Optional alternate hook or call to action, when useful.

Do not claim that audio was rendered, synthesized, uploaded, or published unless an available tool actually completed that action.
